# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Detailed Background of the Study
The transition from academic theory to industrial practice is a defining moment in any student’s journey. In the Kenyan educational landscape, particularly within technical and vocational training, the industrial attachment often referred to as a practicum is far more than a mere administrative requirement for graduation. It serves as a fundamental pedagogical bridge that allows students to step out of the isolated environment of the classroom and into the multifaceted, high pressure professional world. The primary goal of this three month phase is to expose students to real world work environments, where they are finally given the opportunity to apply the complex classroom knowledge they have spent years accumulating. This period is also essential for developing professional ethics, building workplace confidence, and gaining hands on experience with technologies and industrial processes that are often too expensive or logistically complex for a standard university laboratory to house.

However, in recent years, the reality of securing these vital attachments has become increasingly disconnected from the academic ideal. As the number of students enrolling in higher education institutions has skyrocketed across the country, the capacity of industrial partners to absorb, mentor, and supervise these students has remained relatively static or has grown at a significantly slower pace. This imbalance has created what many educators now refer to as a "Placement Crisis." Historically, the process of finding an attachment has been a manual, fragmented, and exhausting endeavor. A student is typically left to their own devices, forced to engage in a "door to door" strategy where they physically travel to various industrial zones, armed with dozens of printed application letters, academic transcripts, and official introduction letters from their university liaison office. This process is not only financially draining requiring significant personal expenditure on transport, printing, and meals but it is also profoundly demoralizing. In many tragic cases, these physical applications are lost in the vast "in trays" of overworked HR managers, or students are placed in departments through personal connections where their specific area of study is entirely irrelevant to the work being performed.

Furthermore, educational institutions like Masinde Muliro University of Science and Technology (MMUST) face an equally daunting administrative challenge. Tracking hundreds of students who are scattered across diverse geographic locations, ensuring that they are actually reporting to their assigned work stations, and facilitating the complex paperwork required by the National Industrial Training Authority (NITA) is an impossible task when handled through manual logbooks and physical files. The administrative burden of verifying insurance coverage and managing the tripartite relationship between the student, the university, and the company often results in delays that can jeopardize a student's graduation timeline.

The AISHA project was born from a vision to fix this broken and outdated ecosystem through the strategic application of modern technology. The name AISHA is not just a title but a reflection of our core technical philosophy: AI (Artificial Intelligence) represents the smart decision making at the heart of the system; Student represents the primary user whose professional life we are trying to improve; Hub represents the centralized ecosystem for the players where all stakeholders meet; and A represents the Automation and Attachment focus that drives the platform's utility. By working in close partnership with industry experts from Datum Limited and the academic coordination team at MMUST, we have developed a platform that does not simply digitize the manual process it completely re-imagines it through a lens of intelligence and transparency.

## 1.2 Comprehensive Problem Statement
The current manual system for industrial attachment placement is a relic of a pre digital era that fails to meet the needs of a modern workforce. One of the most critical failures is the "Blind Search" problem, where students spend months applying to companies that either do not have internship programs or have already reached their full capacity. Because there is no centralized database of available opportunities, this results in a massive and avoidable waste of human potential and student financial resources. This lack of transparency also breeds a system where "social capital" or who a student knows within an organization becomes more important than merit. High performing students from underprivileged backgrounds often find themselves overlooked in favor of those with personal ties to hiring managers, creating a profound sense of unfairness in the professional entry phase.

Beyond the search itself, the documentation and regulatory requirements in Kenya create a massive administrative bottleneck. The mandatory NITA forms, professional insurance documents, and institutional introduction letters form a complex paper trail that is prone to loss and error. Manually filling out these documents and physically transporting them for signatures leads to significant delays that often mean a student begins their attachment before their legal and insurance paperwork is finalized. This exposes both the student and the university to significant legal risks and potential insurance liability in the event of a workplace accident.

Once a student is finally placed, the "monitoring and assessment" phase remains dangerously weak. University supervisors are currently expected to conduct physical site visits to every student, a task that is becoming logistically impossible and prohibitively expensive as student populations grow. The traditional physical logbook is another major point of failure, as it is easily lost, damaged, or even falsified by the student without the university’s knowledge. There is no real time way for a university coordinator to know if a student is actually gaining the required skills or even reporting to work until the very end of the attachment period, when it is often too late to rectify a poor placement. Finally, the persistent mismatch between student skills and organizational needs means that companies often complain about the lack of foundational knowledge in their interns, while students feel they are being used for menial tasks that have no relevance to their degree course.

## 1.3 Strategic Objectives and Project Goals
The primary objective of this project was to design, develop, and deploy AISHA as a comprehensive, AI powered platform that transforms the industrial attachment experience into a seamless and intelligent lifecycle. We aimed to build a centralized, multi tenant web ecosystem that serves as a common ground for students, university administrators, and company HR managers alike. A core goal was the creation of an intelligent matching engine that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs), specifically Google Gemini, to perform semantic analysis of student academic records and align them with the specific departmental needs of industrial partners.

We also prioritized the "One Click" automation of regulatory documentation, ensuring that NITA forms and insurance verifications are generated instantly and stored securely. To solve the monitoring crisis, we aimed to develop a Digital Student Logbook that allows for daily activity recording and digital signing with a clear hierarchical precedence. Furthermore, the project sought to integrate a secure financial gateway using the Safaricom Daraja API to handle all administrative and insurance fees seamlessly. Ultimately, we wanted to ensure that every student's professional data is stored persistently and securely in the cloud, providing a permanent and verifiable record of their industrial experience that will serve them long after they graduate.

## 1.4 Justification and Expected Impact
The justification for the AISHA project lies in its potential to radically improve the "School to Work" transition for thousands of Kenyan students. For the student, it removes the financial and physical barriers to finding a placement and ensures that their hard work in the classroom is recognized by the industry. For the university, AISHA provides a high level "Command Center" for attachment coordination, reducing administrative overhead by an estimated 70% while ensuring every student is accounted for and insured. For the industrial partner, the platform provides a pre vetted pool of talent, significantly improving the quality of their internship intake by using AI to identify the "Best Fit" for every department.

## 1.5 Project Scope and Technical Constraints
The scope of the project encompasses the entire lifecycle of an attachment, from the initial registration and data synchronization phase to the final evaluation and certificate generation. However, the project is not without its constraints. The platform is cloud native and therefore requires a stable internet connection for its core AI and file storage features to function. Furthermore, the system’s intelligence is subject to the rate limits and quotas of external APIs like Google Gemini. To mitigate this, we have implemented a robust local fallback strategy using the Ollama model. Finally, the success of the matching engine remains dependent on the accuracy and completeness of the academic data provided by the university during the initial synchronization phase.


# CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW AND THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

## 2.1 The Historical Context of Industrial Training
Industrial attachment is a concept deeply rooted in the traditional apprenticeship models that have existed for centuries. Historically, a master craftsman would take on a young apprentice to pass down specialized skills through direct observation, repetitive practice, and mentorship. In the modern university context, this has evolved into a formalized and regulated partnership between academia and the industrial sector. In Kenya, the National Industrial Training Authority (NITA) was established specifically to oversee this critical process and ensure that the national labor force is adequately trained and competitive. However, while the *goals* of NITA are inherently modern, the *processes* currently used by many universities and companies to achieve them have remained stagnant and disconnected from the digital reality of the 21st century.

## 2.2 The Three Phases of Placement System Evolution
Placement systems have evolved through three distinct technical phases. Phase 1 was the Manual Era, which relied entirely on physical letters, personal networks, and physical notice boards. Phase 2 saw the introduction of Basic Digital Portals in the early 2000s, but these were often little more than "Digital Notice Boards" that were passive and required significant manual searching by the student. We are now entering Phase 3, spearheaded by platforms like AISHA, which move beyond simple data storage to embrace "Intelligent Systems." These platforms utilize Artificial Intelligence not just to store data, but to understand the semantic intent behind a job description and the unique professional potential hidden within a student’s academic transcript.

## 2.3 The Impact of Artificial Intelligence on Professional Matching
Recent academic research has shown that traditional keyword based matching in recruitment often leads to biased or highly inaccurate results. For example, a student might be a perfect fit for a "Digital Marketing" role because of their specific project work and course units, even if the general keyword "Marketing" does not appear prominently in their course title. Large Language Models (LLMs) solve this problem by using semantic embeddings to understand context. By leveraging the Google Gemini API, AISHA can understand that a student who has excelled in "Statistical Analysis" and "Python" is an ideal candidate for a "Data Analyst" role, even if they are technically enrolled in a general Computer Science degree. This "Semantic Bridge" is what fundamentally sets AISHA apart from traditional job boards and professional networking sites.

## 2.4 Digital Monitoring and the Learning Loop
The traditional physical logbook has long been identified as the "weakest link" in the attachment process. Research into e learning and remote monitoring suggests that immediate, high frequency feedback is critical for professional learning. When a student records a daily activity in a digital logbook and receives an immediate "Sign off" or a constructive comment from their supervisor, the learning loop is closed much more effectively than if they had to wait for a monthly physical visit from a university lecturer. The digital logbook ensures that the attachment remains an active learning experience rather than a passive administrative exercise.

## 2.5 Security, Persistence, and the Cloud Native Shift
A major recurring challenge for university portals has been the risk of data loss and system downtime. The move towards cloud native architectures using tools like Supabase for managed databases and object storage ensures that student data is no longer tied to a single physical server that could fail or be wiped during a restart. The AISHA architecture leverages this cloud persistence to ensure that every student's logs, NITA forms, and final certificates are archived safely for years, providing a permanent and verifiable record of their professional journey.

## 2.6 Identifying the Gap: Why AISHA is Unique
While professional networking platforms like LinkedIn or general job boards like BrighterMonday exist, they are designed for established professionals and do not handle the unique regulatory and academic needs of a student industrial attachment. These general platforms do not understand NITA compliance, they do not integrate with University Management Systems for credit verification, and they do not provide the specialized tripartite communication required for a successful attachment lifecycle. AISHA is the first comprehensive solution designed specifically to fill this gap in the Kenyan educational and industrial ecosystem, providing a tailored tool for students, educators, and employers.
